LXG turns training into hands-on games. Each format is a game built around one kind of judgment: checking a request against the rules, tracing a problem to its cause, planning with limits, making the case. The format holds the world and the rules. A scenario holds the story, the people and the numbers, so one format can teach many subjects.

See the formats

Read the evidence, make a call, see what it did

Every format runs the same three beats. The learner is never told the answer before they commit, and the game never makes the decision for them. What changes from format to format is the world and the hands-on action.

Read

The evidence is in the world

Papers on a desk, a chart on a wall, a customer's own words, a ticket in a queue. Looking is free.

Commit

A call that sticks

Stamp it, pull the lever, ship the fix, send the plan. Most calls can't be undone.

Reveal

The world answers

The migration slips, the alert fires late, the deal stalls on day 2. The debrief names the moment that caused it.

Scenarios are data

Names, lines, numbers and right answers live in a scenario file. A new subject is a new file, not a new game.

Checked before it ships

Formats with a check tool refuse to build when a scenario has a broken link, a missing answer or a dead end.

Built for an LMS

Each game packs into a SCORM zip that reports a score and completion. Nothing is fetched from the internet while it plays.

RPG

Walk an office, talk to people, make calls the world remembers

Long role-playing scenarios in an isometric office world. The learner walks between rooms, reads screens and whiteboards, questions people in close-up conversations and decides what to do. Trust, moods and later scenes all depend on earlier calls. It has 29 ready-made rooms, a cast of 16 people and its own scenario checker. Besides the demo, there are three sample discovery visits. You pick your character, then walk a customer's building and find out what hurts, in their own words: Harbor Lane Insurance (security), Redwood Fashion: first visit (observability) and The bill keeps going up (platform data).

Good for: incident response, stakeholder conversations, sales discovery, new-hire scenarios, anything where who you talk to and what you say matters.

Booth

A service window in the style of Papers, Please. People bring paperwork, the learner checks it against the records and a rulebook that grows each day, asks about anything that doesn't agree, and stamps each request Approve or Return. The demo, Deal desk, is a sales discount-approval desk over three days. A second sample desk, Discovery review, checks the discovery work of a sales team: reps bring a proposal and their meeting notes, and the learner decides whether the customer's pain was really found. It runs three days with six people a day.

1. Call next

Someone steps up

A bell rings, papers slide in, the person says why they need the stamp today.

2. Inspect

Pair two things

Any rule, field or line can be paired. Two that agree say "These two match. Nothing to ask."

3. Ask

Hear their side

Some explanations hold up (the customer asked in writing). Most are pressure.

4. Stamp

Approve or Return

The stamp lands, the person reacts, a citation prints if a rule broke.

5. Report

What it led to

Finance returns a deal, a clean deal stalls, a deal closes. Choices come back days later.

On the desk

  • Request form: the thing being stamped. Discount, volume, price, reason, VP signature line.
  • Account record: what the company has on file. "The record wins over the rep's word."
  • Supporting papers: a forwarded email or a usage report. Every word can be paired.
  • Rulebook: tabs by section, "New" on the shelf when a rule arrives.
  • Clock and line: an 8-hour day. When it runs out, anyone still waiting goes home and is named in the report.

The rules grow

DayNew rule
1Over 30% needs a VP signature. The request must match the record. Volume can't be below 30-day average use.
2A discount cap by yearly price (20%, 35%, 45%). A signature does not raise it.
3A renewal can't start more than 60 days early unless the customer asked in writing.

The traps sit on the change: on day 2, a 28% request passes day 1's rules and breaks the new cap.

A worked example: Dan Okafor, day 1

"It's the same deal they had last year. I just need your stamp before Friday."

Request #4471 for Northwind Freight: 38% discount, $310,000 a year, reason "Same as last year", and the VP signature line is empty. The record says the current discount is 22%.

StampWhat happens
Return (right)"Dan came back with Northwind at 25% and a VP signature. Closed."
ApproveCitation: "Rule 1: 38% discount approved without a Sales VP signature." Day report: Finance returned Northwind. Trust 7 → 6.

Scoring and endings

  • Two meters: Deals closed (of 18) and Deal desk trust (starts at 7 of 10).
  • Wrong in both directions: approving a bad deal adds a citation and drops trust; returning a clean deal stalls it.
  • Pressure moments are remembered. Accepting a rep's trip offer on day 1 comes back in the day 2 and day 3 reports.
  • Four ending letters: promoted to deal desk lead, kept on, taken off the desk, or walked out by compliance.

Making a Booth scenario

Type /booth in Claude Code and describe the desk in plain words: who the learner is, what kind of requests come to the window, which rules they must apply, and which mistakes people make in real life. Claude asks a few questions, shows you an answer key of every person and the right call for each, and after your yes writes the days, the rules and the day each one arrives, the people in line with their papers, the mismatches hidden in those papers, and what each wrong stamp leads to.

"Make a Booth scenario for a clinic intake team checking prior authorizations against each patient's coverage. Three days, with a new payer rule each morning."

Checked before you see it

Before the scenario can be played, Claude runs the Booth check. It won't pass until every hidden mismatch can actually be found on the desk that day, every wrong stamp has a citation explaining the broken rule, and every stamp leads to a line in the day report. You then play it in the browser and ask for changes in plain words.

Good for: any job where a request has to match the records and the current rules. Expense and discount approvals, prior authorizations against a patient's coverage, returns and refunds, loan or claims intake, releasing a work order only when the permit and the inspection log agree.

Journey

A pixel-art walk through a fixed stretch of time. The learner prepares with a budget that can't buy everything, then lives through the days answering decision cards while two resources drain. Some choices come back weeks later, with a line that quotes the early warning sign they could have noticed.

1. Prepare

Buy under scarcity

Items give starting energy or budget, block one named future problem, or unlock a better option later.

2. Pace

Slow, Normal, Fast

Fast burns energy and draws more surprises. Slow restores energy but drains budget.

3. Decide

Cards stop the clock

3 or 4 options, each with its own price in energy or budget. Surprise cards arrive too.

4. Come due

Weeks later

"Because you chose…" plus the sign the learner saw on the day they chose.

5. Debrief

A named ending

Every decision replayed, and which prep item would have prevented each bad one.

One choice, followed through

Day 1, "First Week". The learner picks Skip the meetings, deep-dive into the codebase instead: −6 energy, +8 reputation, −3 team morale.

"Half the team still hasn't spoken to you. You don't know which half."

About three weeks later, Silent Resignation: one of the strongest engineers accepted another offer. Team morale −15, reputation −5. The debrief adds: "Meet-the-Team Dinner would have prevented it." Good choices pay back the same way: handing meetings to a strong lead returns energy weeks later.

Running either one to zero ends the demo early. Always-fast burns out most runs; always-slow hits a spending freeze. Steering between them is the skill.

Making a Journey scenario

Tell Claude the stretch of time, who the learner is, what they have to manage, and which early shortcuts tend to cause trouble later. Claude writes the prep items, the decision cards with their trade-offs, the problems that come due weeks later with the warning sign that came first, and the endings.

"Make a Journey scenario for a new charge nurse's first 60 days. Staff energy and unit budget, and shortcuts on handoffs that come back as incidents."

Checked before you see it

Claude runs the Journey check, which catches anything that would break mid-game, such as a prep item that unlocks a choice that isn't there. You then play it through and ask for changes.

Journey reports to the LMS when the run ends. It doesn't resume partway on purpose: random surprise cards mean a half-finished run can't be rebuilt, so a learner who leaves starts over.

Good for: any stretch of time where early choices come due later. A first 90 days, a product pilot, a store opening, a new charge nurse's first quarter, a plant line changeover where skipped checks show up weeks later as incidents.

From an idea to a course in the LMS

Nobody edits code. You describe the training to Claude Code, opened in the LXG project, and it writes the scenario, checks it, and packages it.

1. Ask"Make a game about returns fraud for store managers." For the RPG, type /rpg; for Booth, /booth.
2. Answer questionsClaude asks who the learner is, what good looks like and which mistakes are common.
3. Claude builds itIt writes the people, evidence, choices and endings, then runs the format's check.
4. Play and adjustPlay it in the browser. Ask for changes in plain words: "make Dan pushier."
5. UploadAsk for the SCORM package and upload the zip to the LMS.

Three skills do the work

The rpg skill walks through designing an RPG scenario, and the booth skill does the same for a Booth desk. The make-scenario skill starts it all: ask Claude to "make a scenario about" something, and it takes your learning objectives, recommends a format and builds the scenario.

What the LMS gets

A score, completion and pass or fail. Most formats also save progress so a learner can leave and come back.

No internet, no live AI

Every picture, font and sound is inside the zip. Everything a learner reads was written and checked before publishing; nothing is generated while they play.

How it plays

Walk, look, talk, then make a call

The learner arrives with a situation and a goal, never with instructions. Arrow keys, WASD or a click to walk. E to use whatever is in reach. Every clickable thing is also a real button for keyboard and screen reader users.

READ

Find the evidence in the world

Screens, logs, whiteboards and people hold the facts. Looking is free.

COMMIT

Make a call

Pick a reply, restart a server, push someone. Each move takes a turn when the stakes are real.

REVEAL

The world answers

Trust moves, a queue fills, a door unlocks. Later scenes can name the moment that caused it.

Encounters

Moments with stakes

Most of a scenario is walking and talking. At the big moments a conversation or a piece of equipment gets stakes: a turn limit, meters such as trust or a filling queue, and its own endings. Every one of them can be lost in a realistic way.

Persuade

Change someone's mind

The person raises concerns one at a time. Each of their lines is one node in a dialogue tree with two to four replies. One reply fits; the rest are mistakes real people make: pressure, blame, half the evidence. A reply only appears once the learner has seen what it rests on.

Probe

Find something out

The person knows things they haven't told you. Good questions open them, in any order, and each answer lands in the notebook. Pushing too hard ends the meeting early.

Diagnose

Work out what is broken

Equipment with evidence screens, actions and a damage meter that rises every turn. Looking is free; every action takes a turn. Fix it under the limit, or fix it late and live with the damage.

Nine recipes built from the same pieces

Turns, meters, concerns, hidden facts and endings combine into a recipe per moment: the three above and six more.

Memory, consequences and XP

The world remembers every call

Underneath everything is one memory: a list of keys and numbers. What you saw, who you met, which reply you picked, each person's trust. Any line, door, person or ending can depend on it.

Consequences name their cause

Any line can name the earlier moment behind it. If someone walks out, the results card says which call lost them. There is never a “wrong” pop-up.

Moods last

Every person has a mood their portrait always shows. Blame someone's team and they are still annoyed when you come back, until you apologize or fix what they care about.

XP for working things out

XP fires only on discoveries and outcomes the scenario marks, never on clicks or walking. XP earned out of XP possible is the score sent to the LMS.

Levels open new things to say

A level gives a point in a skill the scenario names. Skills open lines, details or routes. A skill never wins by itself: an opened line still misses at the wrong moment.

The world

29 ready-made rooms

Rooms are laid out ahead of time on a half-meter grid from a library of 188 pre-rendered pieces in five settings. The format works out the walk map, draw order and door spots from the layout. A scenario picks rooms, opens the doorways it needs, and puts its own things on the spots each room marks: screens, whiteboards, and the company name on signs and logo walls. No room has a brand painted in. Background people fill the seats.

25 close-ups

Every conversation and every piece of equipment opens in a close-up: a painted background, both portraits large, and live screens drawn from scenario text. Screens, logs, chats and dashboards need no art.

Characters

A cast that any scenario can name

Twenty-nine characters and eight player looks. Each walks in eight directions and has six portrait moods, facing sideways for close-ups and facing front for video calls. The role below is only what the look suits: a scenario gives each person their own name and job. The last five began as background people and were promoted to the cast.

Player looks

Eight looks, each in a black Splunk tee with its own line on the front. One set look, or the learner picks at the start. Look, level and skills carry across a series.

Background people

Twenty extras sit at desks, listen in audiences and stand around with coffee. One line in a scenario turns them on. They can't be clicked, and the seats near story people stay empty.

Music

Nine music packs

A scenario names one pack, the way it names a room setting. Every pack fills the same four slots, plus a win sting and a lose sting for the results card. The format picks the slot from what the moment uses, never from the recipe name. Press play on any track to hear it.

Tracks loop in the game. Here each one plays through once, and the stings play once as they do on the results card. Only the pack a scenario names ships in its package.

Making a new scenario

From a learning goal to a playable game

Type /rpg in Claude Code and say what the game should teach. Claude asks the design questions, writes the story with you, builds it and tests it. Nothing new is drawn for a game: the rooms, people, close-ups and music all come from the library, so a new game is writing, not production.

Answer the design questions

What the learner should be able to do by the end, and the shape of the game.

Agree a short outline

Who the player is, the people, the rooms, the big moments and which recipe each uses, how it can end.

Write the story script

Scene by scene: the intro card, a people table, the task list, every dialogue tree node by node, every ending.

Claude builds it

The approved script becomes the game. If the script asks for something the format can't do yet, Claude stops and says so instead of working around it.

Checked automatically

The scenario check tries every possible path through the game. An automated playthrough then plays every route and ending and screenshots each one.

Play it and ask for changes

You get the game running in your browser, with a map of every choice and where it leads. Ask for changes in plain words.

The design questions

  1. What should the player be able to do by the end?
  2. How long is it? One room and one big moment, or chapters.
  3. Who is the player? Their own job, or a role the story needs.
  4. Which player look, or let the learner choose?
  5. One scenario, a series with a menu, or chapters?
  6. Levels or not, and which few skills?

What a scenario describes

Places

Which library rooms it uses, their doorways, locked doors, and the things in it: what each shows, its actions, its live screens.

People

A look from the cast, a name and job, where they stand, starting mood, which conversation they open based on memory, and where they walk later.

Conversations

Dialogue trees. Add stakes for an encounter: turns, meters, concerns or hidden facts, and endings.

Diagnoses

The broken thing, its evidence, the damage meter, and when it counts as fixed.

Tasks, XP and levels

The goal line, the checklist, XP awards on discoveries, level thresholds and what each unlocks.

Endings, music, sound

Results text per ending, retry points, the pass score, the music pack, sounds on specific lines.

What you tell Claude

“/rpg Make a game for new account managers. A customer's renewal is at risk after an outage. They need to hear out each person who was affected, find what each one cares about, and agree a plan with the IT director.”

What the check makes sure of

It tries every situation a learner could be in and fails the scenario if any of them leaves the learner stuck. Every big moment and every ending can be reached, every problem can be fixed, and the largest save fits easily in the LMS. A scenario that fails the check can't be packaged.

Packaging and the LMS

One SCORM zip per game

Each game ships as one SCORM 2004 package that runs in any standard LMS, with no server behind it. The build keeps only the art, music and sounds the scenario uses, so the zip carries nothing extra.

Saved after every actionInto the LMS, or into the browser when played outside one. Close the tab mid-conversation and it reopens there.
Score is XP earned out of XP possiblePassed when the score reaches the scenario's pass score. Completion is reported at a real ending.
Retry without gaming the scoreA failed ending offers to try again from the call that lost it. The run rewinds to that moment; XP can't be earned twice, and nothing goes to the LMS until a real finish.
Fully offlineNothing loads from the internet and no AI runs while learners play. The automated playthrough fails on any request that leaves the package.
Series carry overSeveral scenarios can ship in one package, as a menu or as chapters. The player's look, level, skills and key choices carry into the next one.

Sound and access

Music, sound and accessibility

Music

Nine packs, each with title, explore, encounter and pressure tracks plus win and lose stings. Hear every pack.

Sound

Nineteen library sounds a line can name: a phone ringing, a rack door, fans spinning up, applause. The format adds its own for XP, level-ups, unlocking and menus.

Accessibility

Every clickable thing is a real button. Every state is a word as well as a color, and every meter says what it means in words.
